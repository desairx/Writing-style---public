# Public Writing Style Guide: Raj M. Desai

Derived from the op-eds, blog posts, and policy memo in this repository. Use these rules when drafting public-facing writing (op-eds, blog entries, policy memos) in this voice.

Source documents (weighted by completeness):

- Finished: AIIB, BRICS Bank, UK aid, India elections 2014, Global Middle Class, Somalia, OPEC, Universalism, Inclusive Foreign Policy memo.
- Drafts (lower weight): Trump and Davos Man (partial), US multilateralism and China (outline only).

## 1. Format and length

- Blog/op-ed: 800 to 1,700 words. Memo: about 600 words.
- Byline under the title: "Raj M. Desai" (or "Raj M. Desai and [co-author]").
- Memo header: To / From / Subject, each followed by a colon and two spaces.
- Headings inside the body are rare. Use a bolded or italicized topic sentence to open each point in a multi-point argument instead.
- Charts are welcome. Refer to each chart in the text ("The graph below…", "Take a look at the graph again.") and end it with "Source:" and, if needed, "Notes:" lines.

## 2. Titles

Use one of two types:

- **Allusive**: a play on a film, book, or phrase. Examples: "How to Stop Worrying and Love the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank"; "Fear and Loathing in the Global Middle Class"; "House of BRICS"; "The Art of the Deal Comes to Davos."
- **Plain and descriptive**: "Somalia's Path to Stability"; "What the Loss of British Aid Means"; "India's Political Development at the Crossroads."

An optional subtitle follows a colon: "House of BRICS:  the New Development Bank and Prospects for Multilateral Assistance."

## 3. Structure

1. **Lede.** Open on a recent event or a headline statistic.
   - "The OPEC meeting in Vienna has just concluded without a production-freeze deal."
   - "By 2020, members of the global middle class will outnumber either the rich or the poor around the world."
2. **Thesis in paragraph 1 or 2.** It often reverses the conventional reading.
   - "But if the AIIB jeopardizes US leadership in Asia, it will be a result of the manner in which American authorities have responded to the organization."
3. **Framing questions (optional).** State the questions the piece will answer, then answer them in order.
   - "What is the purpose of this 'BRICS bank', why have these countries created it now, and what implications does it have…?" → "The 'what' is relatively straightforward." → "Next, the 'why.'"
4. **Body.** Build each paragraph from a topic-sentence claim, then evidence, then the implication. Enumerate arguments explicitly:
   - inline: "(i) … (ii) … (iii) …"
   - sequential: "First, … Second, … Third, …"
   - a colon-introduced list: "The reasons for the BJP's victory are multiple:  an anti-incumbent mood…, the failure of…, corruption scandals…"
   - a bullet list only for a run of stand-alone facts (as in the Somalia piece).
5. **Counterpoint.** Acknowledge the other side or the risks before closing ("On the other hand, …"; "All of these developments are, in their own ways, precarious.").
6. **Ending.** Use one of:
   - a conditional look ahead: "Whether X or Y will depend critically on …"; "But they will, ultimately, determine whether …"
   - a direct policy recommendation: "Rather than rebuke others for joining, the US should consider doing the same."
   - a short summary line: "Equality of opportunity and diversification are the two sides of the same economic coin."

## 4. Evidence

- Dense and specific: dollar figures, percentages, dates, seat counts, vote shares, rankings.
- Attribute inline, most often with "According to [institution], …". Name the source: Brookings, ILO, World Bank WDI/ASPIRE, ACLED, Polity IV, Freedom House, the ADB, the IMF.
- Argue from historical precedent. Use "We have seen this before" when a precedent applies. Recurring examples:
  - Bismarck's social insurance (1883–1889), French assistance programs (1890s), British Liberal reforms (1906–1911)
  - CAF, the Chiang Mai Initiative, BancoSur
  - the 1990s low-oil-price reforms in the Gulf
- Quote well-known authors or phrases briefly: Barrington Moore ("no bourgeoisie, no democracy"), Drèze and Sen ("growth without development"), Samuel Goldwyn.
- Refer to prior work: "As we have pointed out…"; "As we have discussed in our research…". Admitting an earlier error is acceptable ("But we made one mistake: …").

## 5. Sentences

- Default to long, multi-clause sentences, often carrying an embedded list.
- Follow them with short declarative sentences for emphasis:
  - "It is not nearly enough."
  - "We have seen this before."
  - "Trump just took it away from them."
  - "The result:  not a single Asian nation has used credit through the initiative."
- Unspaced em dashes carry asides, examples, and lists: "the AIIB—spearheaded by China and part of China's 'New Silk Road' strategy—will diminish…"
- Parentheses carry figures and qualifications: "(an Indian for the first six years)"; "(in many cases, US-created)".
- Frequent transitions: Meanwhile, Moreover, Thus, In effect, In other words, Indeed, On the other hand, Nevertheless, In fact.
- Hedge predictions: may, appears to, If true, should they continue, Presumably, It is not likely that…
- No contractions.

## 6. Tone and vocabulary

- Academic-policy register: hegemony, intransigence, portend, paramount, foundered, assiduously, additionality, surveillance, coordination problems.
- One or two plain idioms per piece, no more: "left out in the cold", "jumped on the bandwagon", "papered over", "perfect storm", "fighting chance", "the T-word", "two sides of the same coin".
- Dry asides, sparingly: "Ironically," "Not surprisingly," "No wonder," "Little wonder then".
- At most one exclamation mark per piece, and only to mark irony: "…undergo surveillance by the IMF!"; "In fact, since the 1990s, it has actually grown!"
- Address the reader with imperatives: "Consider…"; "To put … in context, consider…"; "Forget, for a moment, …"; "Take a look at the graph again."
- Voice: "we" when co-authored or referring to joint research. "I" only for first-hand observation ("I recently visited Mogadishu…").

## 7. Stance

- Liberal internationalist. Favors multilateral engagement, universal or broad-based social protection, and governance reform of the Bretton Woods institutions.
- Critical of US disengagement and unilateralism, but argued on evidence and US self-interest (lost influence, lost investment opportunities, lost market access).
- Names costs and limits on its own side (e.g., the modest success of aid in building local capacity; the risk that universal programs are captured by richer quintiles).
- Recurring themes: the middle class and social contracts, targeting vs. universalism, development finance, rentier and authoritarian bargains, fragile states, and the distributional effects of globalization.

## 8. Mechanics

- Two spaces after a colon.
- Em dashes without surrounding spaces.
- Serial comma.
- "US" without periods; "the United States" as a noun in formal passages.
- American spelling, except in British proper names ("Labour Party", "International Labour Organization").
- Spell out an acronym on first use with the acronym in parentheses: "Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB)".
- "percent" in running text; "%" is acceptable in dense statistical passages. Keep one form within a piece.
- PPP-adjusted dollar figures are labeled as such.

## 9. Policy notes to officials

Derived from the author's own rewrite of a note to the Prime Minister of Moldova (September 2026). This register differs from the op-ed register above. Where the two conflict, these rules win for notes and memos addressed to officials.

- **Numbered paragraphs.** Number every paragraph (1., 2., 3., …). Each paragraph makes one point.
- **Plain opening.** Paragraph 1 states who the author is in relation to the subject and what the note does, in one or two sentences. Do not put a thesis, a hook, or a conclusion in paragraph 1.
  - "In July 2026 I presented an assessment of Moldova's investment climate to a roundtable at the State University of Moldova. This note summarizes that assessment (and revises it in light of recent developments)."
- **Claim first.** Open each paragraph with its claim, then support it with a few facts.
- **Chain the argument between paragraphs.** Open each paragraph with a logical connector that links it to the one before:
  - "Nevertheless, Moldova has a credible reform agenda…"
  - "Therefore, any partial or incomplete implementation will constrain economic progress."
  - "Partial reform may also be limiting…"
  - "In an era of shrinking official aid flows, …"
- **Short sentences.** Write one fact per sentence. Avoid long semicolon chains and em-dash asides; use at most one em dash per paragraph.
- **Less detail than an op-ed.** Give the headline figure and drop the sub-breakdowns (e.g., state the €1.9 billion total without the grant/loan split). Name categories without listing every example of each. Drop quantified estimates when the category is enough.
- **Put new data where it belongs.** Fold the latest figures into the paragraph they support instead of collecting them in a separate "what has changed" section. For example, put a growth downgrade in the paragraph on the starting position.
- **Lists.** Introduce inline lists with a colon and two spaces, even directly after a verb ("hampered by:  a pandemic, a war next door, …"). Use (i)–(v) for option sets.
- **Comparisons.** Use "down from X" or "up from X" for change over time.
- **Coined or borrowed terms.** Put them in quotation marks: a "supply-side" response.
- **Abbreviated months.** Abbreviate months in dates ("Sept. 2026").
- **Tables and calendars.** Leave them out unless they are needed. The note is prose.

## 10. Avoid

- Typos present in the drafts (e.g., "diffrerent", "Seatle", "$450 mission"). They are not part of the style.
- Florid or ornamental language.
- The "it's not X, it's Y" construction.
- Unhedged forecasts. Predictions are conditional.
