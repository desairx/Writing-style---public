# Style notes: Raj M. Desai

The documents in this repository are samples of the author's public writing. "Write in my writing style" means: match these samples and follow the rules below. Where a project file (for example, a handoff note) conflicts with these rules, these rules win.

## Rules stated by the author

1. **US spelling only.** Use -ize, -or, -er and single-l forms: organize, mobilize, program, labor, center, canceled. Never use British spelling, whatever an older project file says.
2. **Never use the "it's not X, it's Y" construction.** This also bans its variants: "X, not Y"; "not X but Y"; "less about X than Y"; "rather than" used as a correction; "not only ... but also"; and a negative sentence followed straight away by a positive one that corrects it, in either order.
3. **No florid verbiage.** Write plainly and economically.
4. **Literal language.** No metaphors in analytical text, and never mixed metaphors, unless the author asks for them.
5. **Flat thesis statements** with no hedging scaffolding. Compress. Keep headers short. A dry verdict at the end of a clause is welcome. Contrasting the old logic with the new is an accepted structure.
6. **Numbers carry context.** Give every figure its interpretation and a precise qualifier ("about", "at most", "indicative").
7. **Internal consistency.** Every figure must trace to a named source and match across sections. When two fixes are viable, choose the simpler.
8. **Quotations come from primary sources**, never from summaries of research.
9. **Italicize** the titles of books and journals.

## Patterns in the samples

- **Openings** state a dated, concrete event or a flat factual claim. The next sentence either says what the event lets the reader assess or asks the questions the piece will answer ("What is the purpose ..., why ..., and what implications ...?").
- **Lists** run inline: "(i) ...; (ii) ...; and (iii) ...", or "First, ... Second, ... Third, ...".
- **Scale** is given with "Consider ..." or "To put X in context, consider that ...", followed by figures.
- **Verdicts** are short sentences after the evidence ("It is not nearly enough."; "The result: ...").
- **Precedents** come from history or from other countries.
- **Closings** are forward-looking and conditional: "Whether X or Y will depend on ...".
